1. Overview

An iPhone app for logging practice time with one tap while holding an instrument and showing it to a teacher during lessons. Its two screens—Log and Share—connect daily practice with conversations at lessons.

I handled problem definition, UX/UI design, device testing and refinement, implementation, and presentation materials. I worked with AI on implementation and made the decisions about which screens and interactions to use.

I used the app in my own practice and lessons. My teacher said the content was clear at a glance and asked for more detailed records (paraphrased).

2. Background and challenge

I play tenor saxophone as a hobby and take lessons every other week. When my teacher asked, “How has practice been going?”, I had no concrete way to say what I had practiced or for how long.

Because I wear my saxophone on a strap, writing on paper during practice is awkward. As a practice journal grows, its records also become scattered across more pages.

The practice-timing apps I tried could track time with one tap, but the challenge was presenting those records in a form my teacher could understand at a glance.

3. Design principles

Connect logging during practice to sharing at lessons. I used two requirements to guide decisions about screens and interactions.

Don’t add input during practice

Let me log time with one tap on a category while holding my instrument.

Don’t add editing before showing the teacher

Automatically organize records so I can show what I practiced and for how long as is.

4. Features and design

Put the control and practice time on the same card

Tapping a category starts the timer; tapping another card switches categories, and tapping the same card again stops it. Each card displays today’s time prominently and is highlighted with a bright color and white outline only while timing is active. The control and the place to check what is being recorded are together.

See the practice breakdown at a glance, without horizontal scrolling

To avoid editing before sharing, the app automatically totals records from the previous lesson date. The daily table prioritizes showing every category at once, even if that means smaller text. The aim is to make it possible to discuss how practice time was allocated while showing the screen to the teacher.

Make accumulated practice a reason to keep going

Daily progress and practice accumulated over months and years are made visible. I hope that having a teacher see the effort put into practice can also help motivate continued practice.

Daily | Fill the bar toward a 1-hour target

Today’s practice bar initially uses one hour as its scale. Category times are stacked in color, with the hope that aiming to fill the bar can prompt practice.

Long term | Show daily accumulation with green squares

Each day is one square, and that day’s practice time is represented by a shade of green. The monthly and yearly grids let users see the squares accumulate; the intent is to make growing daily records a prompt to keep practicing.

5. Process

Use research and conversation to define the roles of logging and sharing

I used ChatGPT to research existing apps and organize the challenge and approach. Within the scope of my research at the time, I found time-tracking apps but did not find a sharing method suited to communication with a teacher. I turned the concept clarified through conversation into a specification, then had Codex implement the first version.

Try the first version and fit the controls on screen

I tried the generated first version myself, described UI and UX improvements, and refined it into an updated version. The first version placed timing status and the stop control in a separate area; I combined them on the card and made today’s time prominent. I also explored layouts that kept the control panel within the screen.

The screen was designed with a dark base for continued viewing during practice, while category colors aimed to create a brighter mood.

Make it possible to compare interaction feel by trying it directly

The feel of “moving smoothly and stopping firmly” could not be fine-tuned through words or images alone. I worked with AI to build a helper tool that let me change and try different methods and values. I compared them on a real device, using a stop position that kept the control panel within the screen as the criterion, then chose the browser’s native snap behavior and applied it to the app.

Use independent reviews, and decide for myself what to adopt

I repeatedly used two independent AI sessions: Claude Code identified possible problems and improvements, I reviewed them, and Codex checked the work and applied needed fixes. Since suggestions can move away from the intended UI or UX, I decided what to adopt based on the app’s purpose and how it felt in use.
* I repeated this process about 15 times. The point when AI reviews no longer raised major defects served as one guide for when to pause improvements.

6. Outcomes and next steps

Opening the app at the start of practice became a habit

I installed the app on my iPhone and used it in daily practice, confirming that I could log with one tap while holding my instrument. Opening it at the start of practice became a positive habit. Looking back at the order and allocation of practice that day, and keeping the next lesson date in mind, help me plan my own practice.

My teacher asked for more detailed records

My teacher said the practice content was clear at a glance and that it would be even more useful to record the types of scales as well (paraphrased). These observations come from my practice and lessons with my teacher and reflect my own experience.

The next challenge is balancing detail with easy input

I’m considering subcategories under “Scales,” such as “Pentatonic scales.” I’m still exploring how to keep logging easy. For the sharing screen, I’m also considering a hierarchical display of categories and subcategories instead of the daily table.

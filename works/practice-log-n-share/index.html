1. Overview

An iPhone app for logging practice time with one tap while holding an instrument, then showing the record to a teacher at a lesson. Its logging and sharing screens bring everyday practice into the lesson conversation.

I defined the problem, designed the UX/UI, refined the interactions on a real device, implemented the app and prepared the supporting materials. I worked with AI on implementation and made the decisions about screens and interactions myself.

I used it in my own practice and lessons. My teacher said the content was clear at a glance and asked for more detailed records (paraphrased).

Try the web demo

2. Background and challenges

I play tenor saxophone as a hobby and take lessons every other week. When my teacher asked how practice was going, I had no concrete way to explain what I had practised or for how long.

With the saxophone hanging from a neck strap, writing on paper during practice is awkward. As the pages of a practice journal accumulate, the records become scattered.

The time-tracking apps I tried could start timing with one tap. The challenge was turning those records into something my teacher could understand at a glance.

3. Design principles

I wanted logging during practice and sharing at a lesson to form one continuous flow. Two requirements guided the screens and interactions.

Keep input simple during practice

Record time with one tap on a category while holding the instrument.

Avoid extra editing before a lesson

Automatically organise the records so I can show what I practised and for how long, just as they are.

4. Features and design

Bring controls and practice time into the same card

Tapping a category starts timing; tapping a different card switches categories; tapping the same card again stops it. Each card prominently shows today's time, with a brighter colour and white outline only while timing. The place where I interact is also where I check what I am recording.

See the practice breakdown without scrolling sideways

To remove the need for editing before sharing, the app automatically summarises records since the previous lesson. For the daily table, I prioritised showing all categories at once, even if that meant smaller text. The aim is to discuss how practice time is distributed while showing the screen to my teacher.

Use accumulated practice as a reason to keep going

I made today's progress and the accumulation over months and years visible. I also hope that being able to show my teacher the effort I have put in will help motivate me to continue.

One day: fill the bar, with an hour as a guide

The bar for today's practice initially uses one hour as its upper limit. I stacked category times in different colours, hoping that working towards that guide would encourage practice.

Over time: green cells show daily progress

Each cell represents a day, with shades of green indicating practice time. The grid shows the accumulation over months and years; I wanted the growing record to encourage continued practice.

5. Development process

Define logging and sharing through research and dialogue

I used ChatGPT to research existing apps and organise the problem and approach. Within the scope of that research at the time, I found time-management apps but no sharing mechanism suited to communicating with a teacher. I turned the concept developed through dialogue into a specification, then gave it to Codex to implement the first version.

Try the first version and keep the controls within the screen

I tried the generated first version myself, requested UI and UX improvements and refined it into the revised version. I brought the timing information and stop control, previously shown separately, into the cards and made today's time prominent. I also explored layouts that kept the control panel within the screen.

I used black as the base for a screen that stays open during practice, while aiming for a cheerful feel through the category colours.

Compare the interaction feel myself before choosing

Words and images alone could not pin down the feeling of “moving smoothly and settling neatly”. I built a tuning tool with AI so I could change methods and values myself. I compared them on a real device, using stops that kept the control panel within the screen as my criterion, then chose the browser's native snapping and applied it to the app.

Use independent reviews and decide what to adopt myself

In two independent AI sessions, I repeatedly checked the issues and improvements proposed by Claude Code, then had Codex verify them and apply the necessary fixes. Suggestions could drift from the intended UI or UX, so I judged them against the app's purpose and how it actually felt to use.
＊I repeated this process about 15 times. When AI reviews stopped identifying major defects, I used that as one indication that it was time to conclude this round of improvements.

6. Experience and next steps

Opening the app became part of starting practice

I installed it on my own iPhone and used it every day, checking how easy it was to log with one tap while holding the instrument. Opening the app at the start became a positive habit. Reviewing the order and distribution of that day's practice, and keeping the next lesson date in mind, helps me plan my practice.

My teacher asked for more detailed records

My teacher said the practice content was clear at a glance and that recording the types of scales would make it more useful (paraphrased). These observations are based on my own experience of practice and lessons with my teacher.

Next: more detail while keeping logging simple

I am considering subcategories such as “Pentatonic scales” under “Scales”. I am still exploring how to keep logging easy. For the sharing screen, I am also considering a hierarchy of categories and subcategories in place of the daily table.

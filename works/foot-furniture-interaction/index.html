Takafumi Horiuchi

Foot-Furniture Interaction

October 2026 · Concept-stage solo project

Why do we stub our little toes
even when we know the furniture is there?

This project explores the design of everyday furniture through cognitive engineering. It is currently a concept: no prototype has been built or evaluated.

Focus: What appearance tells us about the lower structure

I sometimes stub my little toe on a chair or bed leg, even when I know the furniture is there. This familiar, painful experience is the starting point for the project.

I want to study the mental models people use to infer the structure near the floor from the parts of furniture they can see. For example, does a tabletop’s outline shape their expectations of where its supports are and how far they extend?

My working hypothesis is that this mental model influences where people think they can walk when they are not looking down continuously. I do not assume that everything is determined at first glance. I want to observe how the model changes as furniture comes into view, people approach it, walk alongside it, and move past it.

Scenario: Walking past furniture

What do people anticipate at each stage, and how do they move? I want to compare the boundaries they expect with their movements to identify opportunities for design.

As furniture comes into view, I want to examine how its overall shape suggests the structure near the floor, and whether its silhouette, visual continuity, and visible supports provide useful cues.

As people approach, I want to observe how their judgments about where they can walk change with distance. I will consider the outlines, contrast, and arrangement of the lower structure.

As people walk alongside the furniture, I want to observe where the boundary they expect differs from the space their feet actually move through. I will explore clearance for feet, recessed rigid parts, and the use of softness and deformation.

As people move past the furniture and it leaves their field of view, I want to examine changes in foot movement and attention. I will consider how the supports and surrounding space relate to their path.

I have not yet determined which stages matter most. The diagram does not assign them equal importance or establish causal relationships. Observations of cognition, attention, and contact will help set the priorities.

Design approach: Making the lower structure easier to anticipate and the furniture easier to walk past

Making the lower structure easier to anticipate means helping people infer the space furniture occupies near the floor from its visible outline and structure. The aim is to support their mental model of that space and their judgment of where they can walk.

Making furniture easier to walk past means adapting its structure and surrounding space to the movement of the body and feet. Even if someone follows the boundary they expect, a rigid part protruding into the path of their foot can still cause contact. I want to explore foot clearance, the placement of rigid parts, and responses to contact through softness or deformation.

The two approaches are complementary: the space people infer from appearance should relate to the space their feet can actually pass through. I also want to examine how to soften an impact if contact occurs, separately from making passage easier. I plan to explore these principles through fabrication and observation.

Design proposals: Form, structure, and materials

I plan to begin with a side table. To make its lower structure easier to anticipate and the table easier to walk past, I will explore recessed rigid supports, a soft perimeter, and more visible support outlines. I will consider how feet interact with legs, aprons, joints, and low panels, alongside the table’s appearance, usability, fabrication methods, loads, and stability.

The table below summarizes design elements to explore, both as cues to the lower structure and as ways to accommodate movement alongside the furniture. These include form and structure, materials, perceptual cues, motion and deformation, and placement and walking routes.

Design elementDirection to explore
Form and spaceLower corners, cross-sections, and clearance for feet. Consider the height and protrusion of all rigid components, not only the legs, and their position relative to the boundary suggested by the visible outline. Moving the entire legs inward is one option.
MaterialsCombine a visible, soft perimeter with a rigid structure. Consider foot movement, responses to contact, durability, and ease of cleaning.
Perceptual cuesSilhouette, visual continuity, contrast, reflections, shadows, and whether supports are visible or concealed.
Motion and deformationExplore components that flex, move aside, or return to their original shape in response to foot movement. Consider softness and deformation both as responses to contact and in terms of whether they increase or reduce space for feet to pass. Check for instability or new points of contact.
Placement and walking routesPlacement near walls or walkways, clearance from other furniture, and paths alongside it.

For example, fabric hanging from the tabletop could create a visible, soft boundary before a foot reaches a rigid support. I would examine how people perceive that boundary and how it responds to contact, including the risk of entanglement or tripping. The form, materials, and dimensions are still undecided.

Exhibition concept: Exploring the relationship between bodies and furniture

After exploring the design directions and building a physical prototype, I would like to exhibit it at an event such as Maker Faire. Alongside its appearance and feel in use, I want to communicate whether the lower structure is easier to anticipate, whether the furniture is easier to walk past, and whether little-toe contact is less likely.

Displaying the furniture alone may not clearly communicate how it relates to the body or make for an engaging experience. I am considering a short minigame in which visitors walk around it and discover its relationship to their feet through play.

For example, sensors could detect light contact with soft surrogate components and show a score indicating whether visitors touched them or passed without contact. Losing points after each contact could help visitors notice differences in form and structure through play.

Visitors could compare a setup modeled on conventional furniture with the proposed design while following the same path in the same space and completing the same task. Displays of contact locations and overlaid walking paths are also options. The sensing method and scoring are undecided. If proximity is measured, I would distinguish it from actual contact.

The experience would not encourage running or deliberately painful collisions. I would assess fall and contact risks beforehand and avoid making it a race. If I record the activity, I would explain the purpose and scope and obtain consent. Observations of where visitors hesitate or make contact could then inform the next prototype.

Next steps: Fabrication, testing, and a furniture series

The project uses cognitive engineering to explore human–furniture interaction, with the aim of making the lower structure easier to anticipate and the furniture easier to walk past.

Through detailed design and fabrication, I want to test how the furniture works in exhibitions and other settings, then investigate its effects in everyday use. I hope to build on those findings to develop a series of furniture people want to use daily. Eventually, I would like people who discover it at an exhibition to be able to buy it and use it at home.

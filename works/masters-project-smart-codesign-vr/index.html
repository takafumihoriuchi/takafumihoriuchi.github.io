Overview

Smart Codesign VR is a platform for assembling interactive things — “smart objects” — in virtual reality and touching them on the spot to see what they do. The player picks three cards, one each for environment, input and output, and defines by hand what happens and when. The finished object is placed in a virtual park, where it can actually be operated and tried out.

It builds on SNaP (The Smart Nature Protagonists), a board game developed by the i3Lab at the Politecnico di Milano. In SNaP, children combine cards to invent smart objects — but what they invent stays a single sentence on paper. This project set out to give that sentence a body in VR: something you assemble with your own movements and can then touch.

It was designed and implemented by a team of four as the final project for the course Advanced User Interfaces (A.Y. 2020–21) at the Politecnico di Milano. The intended users are children aged 11 to 13, with children with neurodevelopmental disorders (NDD) particularly in mind. It was implemented for the Oculus Quest 2 in Unity and C#, and reached a prototype that runs end to end, from choosing cards to operating the object in the park.

SNaP the board game, and the premises of the design

A game in which children are the designers

SNaP is a board game in which two to four children act as “designers” while an adult facilitator plays the “mayor”. The mayor sets a mission at the start, and the children talk it over and combine cards into a smart object that solves it. The aim is to build creativity, and the ability to work with others and put one’s own thinking into words. The game is also described as usable with children with neurodevelopmental disorders for therapeutic purposes.

Environment, input, output — three kinds of card

SNaP has three kinds of card, each with a different role.

Environment cards are the thing being made smart. Tree, bench, swing, water, stone, streetlamp, bin, bicycle, bridge, stairs, school bag — ordinary things found in a park or a street. Not a sensor and not a function, but the object the other two are built into.

Input cards decide what that object senses: button, motion, touch, distance, temperature, humidity, brightness, colour, sound, fire, water level, weather forecast — the sensors, in other words.

Output cards decide what that object does: light up, make sound, vibrate, move, display, record, send — the actuators.

The environment card is needed because the same pair of input and output means something entirely different depending on where it lives. “Make a sound when motion is detected” on a bin becomes “missing the bin makes a sound”; on a streetlamp it becomes “a light that announces someone approaching”. Input and output alone give only a device specification; only with the environment added does it become a sentence about something in the world around you.

Turning a sentence on paper into something you can touch

Until now a SNaP session ended once the smart object the children had thought up was written down as a sentence. At the i3Lab a “physical board” combining a tablet with Arduino was being developed in parallel to build that sentence physically. Smart Codesign VR is another answer to the same problem, with VR as the medium — a direction the research team had not yet tried.

Choosing VR changes two things. One is that children can prototype with the movement of their whole body: grasping, carrying, shining a light, painting with a brush become, directly, the acts of “setting a condition” and “deciding a behaviour”. The other is that what they thought up rises visually in front of them and can be touched on the spot. An abstract sentence becomes a thing with a size and a shape.

Who it was designed for

The main audience is children aged 11 to 13, with an emphasis on children with neurodevelopmental disorders. Conceiving a smart object takes a degree of logical reasoning, so SNaP and this VR version alike assume a certain age.

In therapy for children with NDD, a common goal is building the ability to voice one’s thinking to another person. Difficulty handling abstractions and a limited span of attention are also known. On the other hand, games you can touch, activities done together with others, and activities supported by adults or by technology are effective, and it is known that engagement changes when the experience itself is interesting. Plenty of children find conventional therapy boring and tense.

Care for the space itself

According to the prior findings drawn on, four things matter in making a space comfortable for children with NDD. Sound (for sensitive hearing: insulation and adjustable volume, with quiet, uniform sound preferred); light and colour (bright, saturated colours kept to small areas, over a calm ground of low saturation); spatial organisation (ordered space with clear boundaries is easier to process); and materials (simple, legible, arranged so as not to obstruct movement). A virtual space is also a place where all four are the designer’s to decide.

The constraints of VR as a medium

At the same time VR carries constraints that cannot be ignored: the cost of the hardware, motion sickness, the load on head and neck from the weight of the headset, accidents caused by a blocked field of view, eye strain from long use, and dependency from over-immersion in the virtual world. For children with NDD there is a further concern of their own — that the boundary between real and virtual can blur more easily.

These fed directly into design decisions. Keep the interaction simple while leaving enough resistance for it to teach something. Keep sight and sound clear without making the stimulus too strong. And give the experience a definite end — once the prototyping and the operation in the park are over, the activity is complete. That is also a decision not to design something that keeps you in the virtual world.

What was built — the flow of the experience and its implementation

The four scenes of the experience

The application is made of four scenes: the standby screen, card selection, prototyping, and operation in the park.

1. Standby screen

On launch a title screen appears and goes no further until a controller button is pressed. It is a deliberate hold, so the main part does not begin before the headset is on and the player settled.

2. Card selection — grasp it, put it in the box

In the next scene one card each is chosen for environment, input and output. The way to choose is to grasp the object standing for the card, carry it to the matching box, and put it in. Grasping and carrying rather than clicking is what makes use of the three dimensions VR has. When all three boxes are detected as filled, a spoken prompt sends the player on to the prototyping scene. The order does not matter, but all three must be filled before anything moves on.

3. Prototyping — deciding condition and behaviour with the body

This is the heart of the system. For the chosen input card the player defines “when”, and for the output card “what it does”. The way each is operated is built separately per card, and each is given a movement that only six-degrees-of-freedom tracking makes possible.

Choose “weather forecast” as the input, for instance, and objects standing for sunny, cloudy, rain, thunder and snow appear at hand. Grasp one of them and point the ray extending from it at the target object, and the condition is set. Choose “light up” as the output, and the player grasps a brush, dips its tip in the paint can for a colour, and rubs it on the target to decide the colour it glows. To remove a colour already applied, dip the brush in the “water” can and rub again.

The larger implementation decision was to allow several input-output pairs to be defined side by side. The original plan had only one: “glow red when sunny” and that was the end of it. Making it several means one object can answer several situations differently — “red when sunny, blue in rain, white in snow”. A pair, once defined, can be run and tried before it is confirmed, and if the player does not like it they can go back and change it.

4. The park — touching what you made

Confirm the object and the scene moves to a virtual park. Here the player places the smart object they assembled, brings about the input condition they defined, and sees the output behaviour for themselves. Once the object has worked, a celebration plays and the experience ends.

Hardware and tools

The target device is the Oculus Quest 2. It has six-degrees-of-freedom tracking, so turning, walking and grasping become input directly. Since handling virtual objects with the hands, over and over, is the centre of the experience, a three-degrees-of-freedom device with a phone slotted into it was judged not to work.

The game engine is Unity and the language C#; the Oculus Integration package provides the connection to the Quest 2, supplying a common API for VR camera behaviour, rendering and controller input.

Four scenes and thirteen classes

The system is divided into four Unity scenes, corresponding directly to the four scenes of the experience. There are thirteen classes in all, three of them abstract.

The title scene holds only TitleSceneCore, which moves on to the next scene when a button is pressed.

The card-selection scene consists of CardSelectionSceneCore and CardSelectionDetector. The former creates three of the latter and assigns them to the selection boxes for environment, input and output. Each box shares the same behaviour: when an object standing for a card comes near, draw it in and mark that category “selected”. Once all three are filled, the selections are written into the SmartObject class and the scene advances. SmartObject is a static class, so its data is not discarded across scenes. That single point is what ties four scenes into one experience.

The prototyping scene is the centre. PrototypingSceneCore manages the whole procedure centrally, and the behaviour of the cards is expressed as an inheritance hierarchy headed by Card. Below Card sit InputCard and OutputCard, and below those the concrete card classes — Weather, Fire, MakeSound, LightUp. Only the concrete leaves actually carry out the prototyping operations, so the three above them are abstract.

PrototypingSceneCore holds a list of input-condition instances and a list of output-behaviour instances. If the cards chosen were “weather forecast” and “light up”, the first list holds only Weather instances and the second only LightUp instances. One of each is created automatically at the start, and thereafter each press of the add button in the UI creates another. Every frame, all the input conditions in the list are evaluated and the matching output behaviours are called. When the user confirms, isConfirmed becomes true and the assembled behaviour data is written into the static properties of SmartObject.

The interaction scene holds only InteractionSceneCore, which takes care of placing the object in the park, running the input conditions and observing the outputs, and the closing celebration.

What was reached, and what was left

Nine of forty-nine cards made it to implementation

Nine cards were implemented in the prototype. The groundwork — choosing cards, defining conditions and behaviours, managing multiple instances, passing data between scenes — was built to a working state, so adding the remaining cards is in principle a matter of time. Six more cards were designed but did not make it into the implementation.

No evaluation was carried out

No evaluation with actual users was carried out. The development period coincided with remote participation under COVID-19, and neither an in-person workshop built around VR headsets nor a remote substitute could be arranged. What this project can show, therefore, reaches as far as the designed experience holding together in a working form; whether it is easy for children to use, or meaningful in a therapeutic setting, has not been established.

An evaluation, were one to be run, would put the question as: how intuitive is this UI/UX for the work of prototyping? Time to completion, how closely the finished smart object matches the one imagined beforehand, and the number of unintended button presses would serve as measures, and a post-session questionnaire based on the SUS (System Usability Scale) would add quantitative data. Because a headset blocks the user’s view, unselfconscious bodily reactions come out more readily in VR, which makes video recording of the session especially useful. As for age, 13 is the realistic line — where SNaP’s intended range overlaps with Oculus’ safety guidance, which does not assume use below 13.

The work that lies ahead

Among the work that time and skill did not stretch to, the first is remaking the UI/UX. Making the card combinations work took the time, and the visual, auditory and tactile expression could not be worked through. In particular, how far text should be used in a VR UI is worth rethinking. Writing was born to record information on a two-dimensional surface, and a three-dimensional space holds many elements that would be better redesigned as a spatial user interface (SUI). For an audience of children with NDD, interaction by voice is a promising direction.

Academically, three paths were named. One is a comparison with the physical board being developed at the same i3Lab. Prior work has found that the same collaborative game, played in a real setting and in a virtual environment, produced more talk on the virtual side; the same kind of comparison could hold between these two. The second is support for several people taking part at once. Given a name with “codesign” in it, being able to build alongside others inside the virtual space is worth having. The third is standardising the description format for smart objects so they can be carried across platforms.

In closing

What this project built is not a sample of a finished smart object but a place for making one. The three cards — environment, input, output — are carried straight across into the units of assembly: the object, the condition, the behaviour. Grasping, pointing and painting are the bodily actions that tie them together. The sentence born in a board game becomes here something you can move with your own hands, and data that remains after the workshop is over.

Overview

Inside a grand piano, the movement of a pressed key travels through a chain of linkages until the hammer is thrown upward and the motion becomes the vibration of a string. That system of levers — the one that lets a pressed key move a hammer and strike a string — is called the piano action. This project rebuilt the piano action as a 3D model in SolidWorks* and used motion analysis to see how input at the key is converted into the speed of the hammer and the reaction force at the string.

For the analysis, the mechanism of the piano key was modelled in 3D from scratch on the basis of reference images, and two motion analyses were run on that model: one on the relationship between the speed of the descending key and that of the rising hammer, the other on the relationship between the force applied to the key and the force arriving at the string. The values from the simulation were checked against theoretical values derived from the dimensions and the positions of the pivots, to establish how motion and force are transmitted inside the model.

* SolidWorks: a 3D CAD system. At the design stage it can run structural analysis to examine the strength of a part and motion analysis to check how it moves.

From the piano action to a model that can be analysed

The mechanism that turns one keystroke into a struck string

The piano action is the mechanism that converts the downward movement of a key into the upward motion of a hammer. Pressing a key sets the capstan, wippen, jack and repetition lever moving together, lifting the hammer until it strikes the string. What the player sees is a single keystroke; inside it, several parts rotate about different pivots and hand the movement on to one another as they touch.

The 3D model includes the key, hammer, jack, wippen, repetition lever, capstan, damper, damper lever and wire, together with the frame that carries them. Understanding the motion took more than looking at the finished shape: it meant defining which part turns about which pivot, and where it meets the next one.

Building a 3D model from a sectional drawing

Rather than using ready-made CAD data, the outline of each part was traced over a sectional reference of the piano action and modelled in 3D one at a time. The parts were then brought together as an assembly, with the fixed and rotational relationships of the real mechanism set between them. That step turned a model for looking at a shape into a model that could be loaded and made to move.

Checking the constraints as degrees of freedom

Excluding the fixed frame, nine parts can move, which without constraints gives 54 degrees of freedom in total. Fixing the capstan and the wire to other parts removes 12, and constraining the remaining seven parts with rotational joints removes a further 35. The CAD assembly therefore has 7 degrees of freedom, as follows.

54 − 12 − 35 = 7

SolidWorks’ own constraint diagnostics reported the same thing: estimated and actual degrees of freedom both 7, redundant constraints 0. This is not a figure that settles the degrees of freedom of piano actions in general — it confirms that the set of parts and the constraints defined for this project are assembled as intended.

Two analyses — how key input is converted

The behaviour of the model was examined in two separate analyses. Analysis 1 asks how the speed at which the key moves is converted into the speed of the hammer; analysis 2 asks how the force applied to the key appears as a reaction force on the string side. Taking one input–output pair at a time makes the behaviour of a complicated linkage explainable through a geometric speed ratio and the balance of forces on a lever.

The basis for comparison is a theoretical calculation in which the mechanism is replaced by simple rotating elements. If the theoretical and simulated values are close, then at least the motion inside the model follows the relationship the chosen dimensions and constraints would lead one to expect.

Analysis 1 — how far the key’s speed is amplified at the hammer

For the speed analysis, the complicated piano action was simplified into three rigid bodies that pass the movement on as they touch: the key, the intermediate mechanism centred on the wippen, and the hammer. Reading the motion as handed on from ① to ③ in the diagram allows a mechanism of many parts to be calculated as three stages of rotation.

Angular velocity is shared across a single rigid body, so with r as the distance from the pivot and v as the linear speed at that point, v = ωr. Assuming the speed passes to the next rigid body at the contact point without loss, the hammer speed follows from multiplying together the ratio of input radius to output radius at each stage.

vhammer = (r1′ r2′ r3′ / r1 r2 r3) vkey

Input conditions and what was observed

In SolidWorks Motion Analysis, 5 N was applied downward at the end of the key and the linear speeds of the key end and of the hammer were recorded. Immediately after the force began, the peak speeds were 1,027 mm/s at the key and 4,284 mm/s at the hammer. In the simulation, then, the hammer moved about 4.17 times as fast as the key.

Against the theoretical value

The radii measured from the model are r1 = 200 mm, r1′ = 88 mm, r2 = 53 mm, r2′ = 78 mm, r3 = 16 mm and r3′ = 111 mm. Substituting them into the equation gives an idealised speed ratio of about 4.49, which predicts a hammer speed of 4,614 mm/s against a key speed of 1,027 mm/s.

The simulated 4,284 mm/s is about 7% below the theoretical value, but the size of the amplification is close. The difference arises, as set out below, because the theoretical calculation idealises the action as three rigid bodies while the 3D simulation also carries the losses between parts and the deformation of the material.

Analysis 2 — how the force at the key appears at the string

The second analysis applied 15 N downward to the surface of the key and recorded the reaction force appearing on the string side at the moment of the strike. The input at the key, Fkey, rose from 1.0 seconds to reach 15 N, and at the moment the string was struck the reaction force Vstring peaked at 31 N.

This value follows from the balance of a lever, using the arm lengths on the key side and the hammer side.

Vstring = (15 × 195) / 94 = 31.1 N

Taking 195 mm from the input point to the pivot and 94 mm to the point of action on the string side gives a theoretical value of 31.1 N. That is 0.1 N from the simulated maximum of 31 N, so this analysis too came out close to the transfer of force the chosen dimensions would lead one to expect.

Simulation against theory

Two analyses that came out close

In analysis 1, comparing the speeds of key and hammer, the theoretical hammer speed of 4,614 mm/s met a simulated 4,284 mm/s. In analysis 2, comparing the input at the key with the reaction force at the string, the theoretical 31.1 N met a simulated maximum of 31 N. In both analyses the results simulated on the 3D model came out close to the theoretical values obtained from the equations.

Why a small difference remains

The theoretical calculation simplifies the piano action into a mechanism of three rigid bodies and treats motion and force as passing through the contact points without loss. The simulation on the 3D model handles each individual part that makes up the action, so energy lost between parts and deformation of the material appear in the result as well. That difference of premises is what shows up as the small gap between the theoretical and simulated values.

Conclusion

The results of the 3D simulation were confirmed to be all but equal to the theoretical values. The 3D model built from a sectional drawing reproduced the motion of the piano action — amplifying the input at the key into the speed of the hammer and carrying that force to the string — in a form consistent with the theoretical calculation.

What this project involved was not only reproducing a shape. Reading the structure out of a sectional reference, translating the relationships between parts into constraints in CAD, and simplifying the result into a mechanical model suited to the question being asked were all part of one modelling process.
